A notebook, a pen, thirty trades

Write it down before you know
whether it worked.

That sentence is the entire discipline. Your broker already records what you made and lost — that part is free. What no app records is what you were thinking when you pressed the button, and that's the only thing you can actually improve. This page teaches you the sheet, the fields, and the arithmetic to run it by hand.

Why bother

You cannot tell a good trade from a lucky one by looking at the money

Every trade you take lands in one of four boxes. Profit and loss only tells you which column you're in. The journal is what tells you which row — and the row is the part that decides whether you're building a skill or just getting paid by variance.

The one field that matters most

Of everything on the sheet, "Did I follow my plan? Y / N" is the field that earns the notebook its place. It's the only one that sorts the rows. A trader who wins 40% of the time and always follows the plan will beat a trader who wins 60% and improvises — because the first one has something repeatable and the second one has a coin.

Before you start

What this costs

A composition notebook and a pen. That's genuinely it — and paper beats an app here, because the friction of writing by hand is a feature. It slows you down at exactly the moment you most want to move fast, and it makes a bad trade physically annoying to enter.

Rule the notebook once, up front: first page is your rules, next two pages are your weekly reviews, everything after that is trades — one trade per half-page, so you get two per sheet.

Fill in the blanks in your own handwriting, then sign and date the bottom. This sounds theatrical. Do it anyway — the whole point is that these numbers get decided by you when you're calm, not by you at 10:31 on a Tuesday when a position is going against you.

My rules

Decided once · not renegotiated mid-trade
  1. Starting balance: — and this is money I can lose entirely without it changing my life.
  2. Most I will risk on any one trade: % of the account. Pick 1% or 2%. Not 10%.
  3. Most I will lose in one day before I close the laptop: %.
  4. Maximum trades per day: . Boredom is not a signal.
  5. I will not enter a trade until the thesis and the stop are written down.
  6. I will not move a stop further away once the trade is live. Ever.
  7. I will not add to a losing position.
  8. I will not trade when I am angry, bored, drunk, or trying to win yesterday back.
  9. I will log every trade — especially the embarrassing ones.
  10. I will review the whole book every Sunday before I take another trade.
Signed / dated
 
Rule 9 is the one people quietly break. A journal with the bad trades missing is worse than no journal — it will tell you you're better than you are, and you'll size up on a lie.

Why each field is there

The four that do the real work

The rest are bookkeeping. These four are the ones that change your behaviour, and each one is doing a specific job.

  • Thesis, in one sentence before entry

    One sentence, in plain language, naming what you expect and roughly why. If you can't write it, you don't have a trade — you have an urge. This single constraint eliminates most bad entries before they happen, which is the cheapest filter available to you. Later, when you reread thirty of them, patterns show up that are invisible from inside a single trade: which kinds of thesis actually work for you, and which just sound convincing at the time.

  • The stop — "I'm wrong at" before entry

    A price that proves the thesis false, decided while you have no money on the line and no opinion to defend. Writing it down converts an unlimited loss into a known one, which is what makes the R arithmetic below possible at all. The moment you move a stop further away, the trade stops being the trade you planned and becomes a hope, and hopes have no maximum loss.

  • P&L in R, not just dollars after exit

    R is your unit of risk. Risk $10, make $30 — that's +3R, whether the account is $1,000 or $100,000. Measuring in R makes every trade comparable and quietly removes the thing that wrecks people: feeling great about a big win that was actually a reckless bet, or sick about a small loss that was textbook. It also lets you add up a month of trades that were all different sizes.

  • Followed plan? Y / N after exit

    The honest one. Not "was I right" — did I do the thing I said I'd do. This is the field that tells the difference between a losing trade and a mistake, and they are not the same thing. Your percentage of Y answers is the score that predicts whether you'll still have an account in a year. Track it more closely than your win rate.

Arithmetic you can do on the page

Three numbers, no spreadsheet

1. R — your unit of risk

Set it once per trade: entry minus stop, times size. Then every result gets divided by it.

Risk per share = entry − stop = $7.35 − $7.10 = $0.25 1R in dollars = risk × size = $0.25 × 40 = $10.00 Result in R = P&L ÷ 1R = −$9.20 ÷ $10 = −0.92R

2. Expectancy — what one average trade is worth

The only number that says whether your method makes money. Add up thirty trades and work it out once.

Expectancy = (win% × average win) − (loss% × average loss) Book A 30 trades, 12 wins → 40% win rate, avg win +1.8R, avg loss −0.9R = (0.40 × 1.8) − (0.60 × 0.9) = 0.72 − 0.54 = +0.18R per trade Book B 30 trades, 17 wins → 55% win rate, avg win +0.8R, avg loss −1.3R = (0.55 × 0.8) − (0.45 × 1.3) = 0.44 − 0.585 = −0.15R per trade

Read those two again

Book B wins more often and loses money. Book A is wrong six times out of ten and makes money. This is why "what's your win rate" is close to a meaningless question, and why the two fields people skip — average win and average loss in R — are the ones that carry the answer. If your expectancy is negative, no position sizing, no bigger account and no better entries fix it. You are simply paying to play, and the only correct move is to stop and change the method.

3. What your own numbers say about the $1,000 → $10,000 idea

Once you have a real expectancy, this stops being a debate and becomes arithmetic. Assume you turn out to be genuinely good — Book A's +0.18R edge, which most people never reach.

This is the lesson the notebook teaches for free

Even with a real edge, a 10× at responsible sizing takes about five years. The only lever that makes it fast is risking more per trade — and that same lever is what turns a normal run of six losses in a row into a dead account. At 5% a side, six losers back to back is a third of the account gone, and six losers in a row is completely ordinary at a 40% win rate.

So the honest version of the challenge isn't "can I 10× $1,000." It's "do I have a positive expectancy at all?" Thirty paper trades and this notebook answer that, and the answer costs you nothing but a few weeks and some handwriting. If it comes back positive, you've found something genuinely rare and you can size it up slowly with real money. If it comes back negative — which is the common result, and no reflection on you — you just saved the $1,000 and learned more than the trade would have taught you.

This week

How to actually start

  1. Buy the notebook today

    Any composition book. Rule page one with your rules, sign it, date it. Do this before you look at a single chart.

  2. Pick a fake starting balance and write it down

    Use $1,000 — the number from the challenge. 1% risk means $10 a trade, which is exactly the R in the worked example above. Everything stays honest as long as you never quietly revise the balance upward.

  3. Take thirty trades on paper, no money involved

    Real prices, real timestamps, imaginary money. Fill the sheet before you "enter" and after you "exit" — and take the losses honestly, including the ones where you'd have panicked. The most common way to fool yourself is a paper trade you'd never actually have held.

  4. Do the Sunday review every single week

    Even on a week with two trades. The habit is what you're building, more than the data.

  5. After thirty, work out your expectancy and read it plainly

    Positive with a plan-adherence rate above 90%? You've earned the right to try a small amount of real money — the speculation sleeve, capped, ring-fenced, never rent. Negative, or adherence under 90%? The notebook just did its job. Change the method or put the energy into the overtime and contract streams, which pay about $36 an hour with a 100% hit rate.

One more thing

Keep this notebook separate from the money in the other tool. The paycheck, the Roth and the index fund are the machine, and they work precisely because they're boring and automatic. This notebook is a skill experiment with a fixed, tiny budget and a definite end date. Mixing the two is how people end up explaining to themselves why the emergency fund is temporarily in a options position.

The example trade, the two books and every figure in the tables are worked examples built to demonstrate the arithmetic — not predictions, and not a strategy to copy. Your numbers will be your own, which is the entire point of writing them down.
